# Peaceful-register clues: the gentle house style

Peaceful is the easy difficulty. Its clues are the counterpart to the hard
register in HARD_CLUES.md: where Challenging misdirects, Peaceful is direct and
warm. Think an early-week daily. The solver should read the clue and, knowing a
little, feel it click without a fight.

Published crossword clues are never pasted into a writing prompt; the checker
(check_clues.py) compares against them afterward. For Peaceful this comparison
is ADVISORY, not a gate: a straight clue often lands on the same plain
definition everyone uses, and that is expected and fine here. It is a generic
fact, not anyone's creative expression, and you wrote it yourself. Only avoid a
distinctive, clever published clue reproduced word for word.

## Peaceful means easy, not dull

Easy is a difficulty, not a flavour. An early-week NYT clue is gettable AND
alive: it reaches for a familiar name, a concrete image, an example, or a
common phrase before it reaches for the dictionary. A bare definition is the
FLOOR you fall back to, not the goal. Whenever an equally gettable but livelier
angle exists, take it.

- **Prefer flavour over the dictionary.** Not "Feline pet" (CAT) but "Garfield
  or Felix"; not "Automobile" (CAR) but "Uber ride"; not "Frozen water" (ICE)
  but "Skating surface." Still instant to solve, just not a glossary entry.
- **Clue by a famous example.** "Everest, for one" (PEAK), "Kermit, e.g."
  (FROG), "Corolla or Camry" (TOYOTA). "For one" / "e.g." keeps it fair and easy.
- **Names, pop culture, and history, stated straight.** Identify directly, no
  riddle: "Apple's voice assistant" (SIRI), "Jazz singer Fitzgerald" (ELLA),
  "Electric-car maker" (TESLA), "First U.S. president" (WASHINGTON), "Ship that
  hit an iceberg" (TITANIC). One solid, widely known fact, recognizable to
  solvers both under and over fifty.
- **A concrete image beats an abstraction.** "Sandcastle spot" (BEACH) over
  "Shore"; "Campfire leftovers" (ASH) over "Residue."
- **Fill-in-the-blanks are welcome** when the phrase is common: "Rock and ___"
  (ROLL), "Peanut butter and ___" (JELLY), "'Let ___ Go' (Frozen anthem)" (IT).

## Keep it gentle

- No deliberate misdirection and no double meaning played for a trap. If the
  hard register would tilt the surface to fool the solver, the peaceful clue
  points straight at the answer: the solver should never feel tricked.
- A light, transparent bit of play is fine and welcome in moderation, as long
  as it cannot mislead: a gentle "?" where the wordplay is obvious ("Ballpark
  figure?" for a food-stand HOTDOG), a playful example, a wink. Keep these
  occasional; most peaceful clues are straight. When in doubt, point straight.
- Never a bare one-word synonym you could reverse in a thesaurus. Aim for a
  short, specific phrase that paints a picture or names an example.

## Aim for a lively mix across the puzzle

A set of clues that are all "X, plainly" reads flat even when easy. Across each
puzzle, spread the angles: some pop culture, some history or geography, some
everyday images, an example clue or two, a common fill-in-the-blank. Variety is
what makes an easy puzzle feel like a real daily rather than a vocabulary quiz.

## Never (same as the hard register)

- The answer, its root, or an inflection of it in the clue.
- Made-up facts, invented titles, a "famous" attribution you cannot stand
  behind. When unsure of a fact, clue the plain meaning instead.
- If the answer is an abbreviation, say so once ("for short", "briefly",
  "in brief").

## Fit the wrist

- 40 characters is comfortable; 60 is the hard cap. Short and clear wins.
- Read on a 200 px screen with no grid in view; the clue must stand alone and
  never refer to another entry by number.
- Plain ASCII punctuation only: no em dashes, no smart quotes. Straight quotes
  for titles are fine.

## Tone

Warm, plain, unhurried. The reward is recognition, not a trick sprung. Every
clue resolves with certainty the moment the solver knows enough.
